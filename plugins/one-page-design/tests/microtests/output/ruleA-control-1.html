Merge-and-sell economy: does it punish impatient picks?

Design note for production. Rules as specified: 8 slots, about 9 choices per run, one action per win (take one of three, or remove one), three-of-a-kind merges at x1.5 rounded up, no currency.

Short answer: yes, and the "one ninth" figure understates it. Undoing a wrong pick costs 1 choice of 9 (11%), but the wrong pick itself already spent a choice, and the emptied slot needs another choice to refill. Counted against a run with no mistake, a pick that is taken and then undone costs about 2 of 9 choices (22%), and a pick that is kept costs 1 of 9 plus a slot. The sharper effect is a cliff, not a flat tax: a single-piece build that wants two merge tiers uses all 9 choices, so one wasted choice removes the second tier.

What the claim gets right

  • Removing a piece is a full action. It cannot be combined with a take, so the undo consumes exactly 1 of about 9 choices.
  • A removed piece becomes a Blank, and a Blank does nothing. The slot contributes nothing until a later take fills it.
  • There is no currency, so nothing offsets the loss. No refund, no trade, no bank.

What the claim misses

  • Three choices, not one. Wrong take (1), remove (1), correct refill (1). The correct piece would have cost 1 anyway, so the net loss is 2.
  • Blank lasts at least one fight. Removal happens at a reward. The next fight is played with that slot Blank. The refill can come no sooner than the next reward, and only if the three offers include a build piece (offer odds are not specified).
  • The wrong piece also sat in the slot for the fight before. It did something, possibly harmful, possibly useful. Not modeled here.
  • Undo is sometimes optional. If a free slot exists, leaving the piece costs no removal. Early on this is common; see slot pressure below.

Slot pressure (why undo is often optional)

Eight slots against about nine choices looks tight, but each merge frees two slots. A focused build keeps filling and collapsing, so it rarely runs out of room. A scattered build does: 8 unmerged singles fill every slot, and then every further take needs a removal first.

Implication: the punishment is mild for a focused player who slips once, and compounds for a player who keeps sampling. Impatience is punished by repetition more than by any single pick.

What softens the cost

  • Visible opponents. Seeing rosters, merged pieces included, lets a player judge the build before committing. This is the main defense against the "sounds good" pick, and it works only if the player reads it.
  • Round-up on merges. Small numbers gain most (1 becomes 2, 3 becomes 5), so an early merge of cheap pieces recovers some ground.
  • A wrong piece may still fight. Kept in a spare slot it may carry weight until the end.

Verdict for the producer

The economy does punish impatience, in a clean and legible way: no hidden currency, one visible cost. It punishes harder than the stated 1/9. If the aim is "a mistake hurts but does not end the run", a 2-of-9 cost with a one-fight Blank sits near that line. If the aim is a gentle rebound, the second-merge cliff is the part to soften.

Open questions and assumptions

  • Offer rules: how often do the three offers include a piece that fits the build? This sets Blank duration.
  • Is the choice count fixed at 9 or "about"? The cliff sits exactly at 9; at 10 or 11 one slip is survivable.
  • How much does a Blank cost in a fight? Not specified, so the fight impact is unquantified.
  • Figures above are counting arguments from the stated rules, not playtest data. Worth a simulation or a paper run of ten players before tuning.
