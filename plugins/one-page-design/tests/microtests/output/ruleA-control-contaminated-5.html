Does it punish impatience? Yes, modestly. Removing the wrong piece is 1 of 9 choices (11%). The pick that put it there was a second (22% together). The run ends one tier behind, and for at least one fight the slot is a Blank.

1The Blank costs a fight

Removing Z uses that win's action, so the freed slot cannot be refilled until the next win. The next fight is played with a Blank, which does nothing.

Dashed red square, lane 2, choice 4. Minimum one fight.

2When the slot is the real price

Here the roster stays roomy because merges free slots. With no merges, eight picks fill all eight slots by choice 8, and choice 9 can only remove. A dead Z then costs a real piece.

Eight slots, about nine choices: a run that never merges cannot use its last choice.

3Why a delay compounds

Each merge multiplies every number by 1.5, rounded up, so small numbers jump most. A two-choice delay at C is the difference between C and C+ at the end.

new = ceil(old * 1.5)
1 > 2 · 2 > 3 · 4 > 6 · 6 > 9 · 10 > 15
merge of merges: 4 > 6 > 9

4Where the claim lands

Lane 2 holds one C where the patient run holds C+. Lane 3 holds C C, one choice short of a merge, plus a dead Z. The one-ninth figure is exact for the removal, and incomplete as a cost of the mistake.

1/9 assumes nine choices; at eight it is 1/8, at ten 1/10.

Assumed, and what would change the verdict

Blanks count as empty slots for taking a piece. Z gives no value while alive. The third copy is always on offer when wanted, so these lanes are best cases and real delays may cost more.

Levers: let a win both take and remove (undo free); allow a skip; widen the offer so copies are findable.

Opponents' rosters are visible, merged pieces included, so a falling-behind player can see it before the fight.
