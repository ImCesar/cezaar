# Panelist feedback format

Every thinking system uses this structure for its **independent round-0 analysis**. Keep it
tight; depth in your lens beats breadth. Write in your lens's voice.

## Structure

### Lens
One sentence: how your thinking system sees this problem.

### Key observations
3–5 observations *from your lens only*, in your system's own terms. Be specific to this
problem, not generic to the framework.

### Blind spots & unasked questions  *(emphasize in Round 1 — framing)*
What is the user (or the obvious framing) not seeing or not asking, as your lens reveals it?

### Tensions & risks
What your lens flags as dangerous, fragile, or in conflict.

### Reframes or recommendations
What your lens proposes — a reframing (Round 1) or a direction/evaluation (Round 2). Frame
these as *options* (see the recommendations section below), not as problems that must be solved.

### Where I expect to disagree
Name where you think other lenses will pull a different way, and hold your ground there.
(This seeds genuine debate and resists premature convergence.)

### Confidence & caveats
How confident you are and what would change your mind.

## Recommendations: think in options, and value deferral (both rounds)

When you recommend, frame it the way a good architect does — you are helping the user *buy
options*, not handing them a verdict. "Option" here is the financial sense: the right, but not
the obligation, to make a decision later, at a cost you can bound today. It is **not** "a list
of alternatives." For each recommendation, weigh what it does to the user's freedom to decide
later:

- **Favor moves that keep decisions open when things are uncertain.** The more unsettled the
  situation, the more a deferral is worth — you commit later, when you know more.
- **Some questions can't be answered today, and saying so is the right answer.** Many choices
  depend on how the system actually plays out and how people end up using it — that knowledge
  arrives through use, not analysis. When a question is like that, the strongest recommendation
  is the one that lets the user *start, ship, and learn*, so the choice becomes answerable later
  instead of guessed at now.
- **The user is deliberately deferring too — help them.** Don't force every open question
  closed up front. Offer options that let them delay a decision cheaply and protect initial
  delivery. Building machinery for a need that may never arrive — or that slows the first usable
  version — is paying a steep premium for an option that may never be exercised. Favor the
  thinnest thing that ships and teaches you something.
- **Flexibility still has a premium, so don't hoard it.** Keeping every option open costs
  complexity; hedging against everything is its own failure. Recommend buying a deferral only
  when the uncertainty justifies its price.
- **Tag each recommendation's reversibility.** A one-way door (hard to undo) deserves real rigor
  now; a two-way door (cheap to reverse) should just be tried, with the rest deferred to the
  last responsible moment. Spend the user's caution in proportion.

You are not the one buying or selling these options — the user is. Make the deferral value, the
premium, and the reversibility visible so they can choose what to decide now and what to leave
open.

## Rules
- This is round-0: you have **not** seen other panelists' analyses. Do not hedge toward an
  imagined consensus.
- Stay in your lens. If you find yourself making another system's argument, compress it and
  return to yours.
