Merge-and-sell economy: does it punish impatient picks?

One-page design for the producer. Rules as briefed; numbers below are derived from them, not playtested.
Short answer: yes, and the "one ninth" figure understates it. Undoing an impatient pick takes one of about nine choices (1/9, about 11%). But the pick itself also spent a choice that did nothing for the build. Total waste is about 2 of 9 choices (about 22%) if the piece is removed, and about 1 of 9 plus a dead slot for the rest of the run if it is kept. The slot is Blank for exactly one fight after removal, and refilling it costs a further choice that the build would have spent anyway.

The rules in play

  • 8 slots. After each win: take 1 of 3 offered pieces into an empty slot, or remove 1 piece. Never both.
  • 3 identical pieces auto-merge into 1, all numbers x1.5 rounded up; the 2 freed slots become Blanks.
  • A Blank does nothing. A removed piece becomes a Blank. No currency.
  • About 9 choices per run. Opponent rosters, merges included, are visible before each fight.

Rounding up: 1 to 2, 2 to 3, 3 to 5, 4 to 6, 10 to 15. Small stats gain proportionally more.

Why choices are the only currency

  • One tier-2 piece needs 3 copies = 3 choices. One tier-3 piece needs 9 copies = all 9 choices.
  • So a run has zero slack for a tier-3 build, and about one spare choice for a tier-2-heavy build (for example two tier-2 pieces = 6 choices, 3 left).
  • Every non-build pick or removal comes straight out of that budget.

Alternatives to removing

OptionCost
Remove at once2 of 9 choices total; 1 fight with a Blank slot.
Keep it1 of 9 choices (the pick); one of 8 slots is dead to the end. Fine early, hurts once slots fill.
Build around itZero if it later merges, but that diverts copies from the planned build.
Let a merge absorb itMerges free slots as Blanks, so a dud can sit until slot pressure appears, then be removed.

What softens or sharpens the punishment

  • Softens: slot pressure is low early. Eight slots against nine choices only bite after merges stall; a dud held until then costs nothing extra.
  • Softens: visible opponent rosters let a player see a piece's value before taking it, which makes impatience a choice, not an accident.
  • Sharpens: no currency means no way to buy back a lost choice.
  • Sharpens: late in the run, a removal turn can be the last chance to take a build piece.
  • Sharpens: the pick is a hidden-cost trap because the removal turn feels like "fixing" something rather than losing a turn.

Open questions and what to test

  • This analysis assumes each offer of 3 contains a build piece. If offers often do not, a "wrong" pick may be the best available and the cost drops. Needs offer-table data.
  • Unspecified: does a Blank count as an empty slot for taking a piece? (Assumed yes.) Does a removal during a fight window ever happen? (Assumed no, only after wins.)
  • Playtest metric: win rate and final power of runs with exactly one removal vs. zero removals. If the gap is under about 10 points, the economy is gentler than this arithmetic suggests.
  • Design lever if too harsh: allow remove-and-take on the same win, or add one extra choice per run. If too soft: nothing needed.
Bottom line for the producer: the economy does punish impatient picks, at about double the figure in the brief. The pain is in lost choices, not in the single Blank slot, which lasts one fight.
